Dental Practice Financing Canada: 7 Checks Before You Borrow

Dental practice financing in Canada is not just a rate quote. Buyers need lender fit, cash-flow proof, lease security, structure advice and closing conditions lined up before a purchase offer becomes hard to unwind.

By Dental Broker Team - Published September 15, 2026

Direct answer: line up financing before the perfect listing appears

The practical way to approach dental practice financing in Canada is to qualify yourself, the target practice and the deal structure at the same time. A strong buyer does not wait until after a letter of intent to ask whether the bank likes the lease, whether projected cash flow supports the debt, whether the corporation can borrow, or whether the seller expects an asset sale or share sale. Those questions shape what you can offer.

This article is for dentists and associates comparing practice purchase financing, not for sellers preparing a sale file. If you are earlier in the acquisition process, start with the first-time buyer guide. If you are reviewing public opportunities, the guide to Ontario dental practices for sale explains how listing signals should be treated before confidential diligence.

Dental Broker Team works with Ontario dentists on buyer conversations, appraisals and transition planning. Treat this as educational guidance only. Financing, tax, legal, insurance and professional corporation decisions should be confirmed with qualified advisors and the lender reviewing your file.

7 dental practice financing checks before you borrow

1. Get lender-ready before you request confidential files

A serious buyer should speak with a healthcare or professional banking lender before making inquiries on specific practices. The lender conversation should cover your personal debt, credit history, savings, income, clinical experience, desired geography, purchase range and timeline. This is not the same as final approval, because approval depends on the target practice and deal terms. It does help you avoid chasing opportunities that do not fit your financing capacity.

Ask lenders what they need for a preliminary view. You may need personal financial statements, tax returns, student debt details, proof of professional standing, resume or production history, cash available for closing costs and a basic acquisition plan. If a lender's answer is vague, ask what would slow approval once a practice file arrives.

2. Test practice cash flow against total debt service

Practice financing should be modeled from available cash flow, not just purchase price. Look at normalized owner earnings, associate costs, hygiene strength, rent, payroll, supplies, lab fees, equipment spending, software, insurance, advisory costs and expected working capital. Then test the proposed loan payment, any vendor take-back financing, personal debt obligations and the salary you need to live.

A seller's asking price may still be defensible, while the buyer's personal debt profile makes the deal uncomfortable. That is why lender review, accounting advice and a sober buyer budget need to happen together. The article on how to value a dental practice in Ontario can help separate value evidence from affordability.

3. Confirm the lease can support the amortization

For many dental acquisitions, the lease is the lender's practical anchor. If the practice cannot keep operating in the same premises, the goodwill you are financing becomes riskier. Review current term, renewal options, landlord consent, assignment rights, demolition language, relocation rights, personal guarantees and rent escalations.

DMC's financing checklist says banks often want proof of lease assignment and landlord consent, and it flags why term length and demolition clauses matter. Do not treat lease review as a closing-day legal formality. If the lease is weak, financing conditions, purchase price, transition plan and legal structure may all need adjustment.

4. Compare conventional bank financing with program-based options

Canadian buyers may hear about professional banking loans, lines of credit, equipment financing, commercial mortgages, vendor financing and government-supported programs. These are not interchangeable. Some fund goodwill or a practice purchase. Some fund equipment or leaseholds. Some require a business account, specific security or personal guarantees. Some may not fund a share acquisition.

Use the Canada Small Business Financing Program as a checklist prompt rather than a shortcut. Ask whether the target purchase is eligible, whether the bank participates, what portion can be financed, what fees apply and how the program interacts with conventional lending. If the seller expects a share deal, verify eligibility early because program guidance is not the same as ordinary bank policy.

5. Decide whether the borrower is you or a dentistry professional corporation

Where the loan sits can affect tax, liability, security, repayment and required documents. A buyer may borrow personally, through a dentistry professional corporation, or through a structure recommended by legal and tax advisors. Cadence's guide argues that assets versus shares and loan location should be considered together, not after the price is settled.

In Ontario, professional corporation ownership and change-of-shareholder documentation can bring RCDSO requirements into the timeline. If the corporation borrows, expect the lender to ask for corporate documents, resolutions, certificates of status and security documents. Do not copy another dentist's structure without confirming that it fits your province, lender and target deal.

6. Understand guarantees, security and insurance before signing

Financing language can feel routine until the deal is under pressure. Ask the lender to explain personal guarantees, spousal guarantees if raised, general security agreements, PPSA registrations, life and disability insurance, fire or property insurance, general liability insurance and any assignment of insurance proceeds. These terms affect your risk after closing, not just the bank's file.

The goal is not to avoid every form of security. Many lenders require security to support the loan. The goal is to understand what you are pledging, when the obligation ends, what happens on default and whether the documents match the purchase agreement, lease and insurance plan.

7. Keep financing conditions tied to real diligence

A financing condition is only useful if the diligence supports it. Before removing conditions, confirm the lender has reviewed the purchase agreement, financial statements, lease, insurance plan, valuation or appraisal support, ownership structure and any material obligations. If the practice depends heavily on the seller's production, one associate, a short lease or fragile hygiene recall, the financing decision should reflect that risk.

Buyer excitement can compress timelines. Keep the file disciplined. A good acquisition process should leave room for lender questions, legal review, accounting review, privacy controls and a transition plan. Common financing mistakes buyers can avoid

Do not assume a bank's advertised maximum applies to your deal. Do not remove financing conditions because the practice is popular. Do not treat a seller's normalized earnings as your personal take-home pay. Do not ignore lease risk because the practice has good production. Do not let tax structure be decided by whoever drafts the first version of the offer.

Also avoid asking for sensitive patient or staff-level information too early. In Ontario, the RCDSO guidance around personal health information and confidentiality matters even when the buyer has serious financing interest. Stage disclosure so the lender and advisors receive what they need without creating unnecessary privacy risk.

How to use this guide with advisors

Bring the seven checks to your lender, lawyer, accountant and acquisition advisor before you are emotionally attached to one listing. Ask the lender what they can support. Ask the accountant to model cash flow and tax structure. Ask the lawyer to review lease, corporation, security and purchase terms. Ask your clinical or transition advisor whether the practice's revenue can transfer to you.

The best financing process gives you a clear yes, no or not-yet. Sometimes the right move is to proceed. Sometimes it is to renegotiate, improve conditions, find a different structure or wait for a practice that fits your risk profile. The goal is not simply to borrow enough. The goal is to own a practice that can repay the debt while giving you a workable professional life.

FAQ

Can dentists finance a dental practice purchase in Canada?

Yes, many Canadian dentists use bank financing, professional banking programs, vendor financing or a mix of sources. Approval depends on borrower profile, practice cash flow, structure, lease security, diligence and lender policy.

What do lenders check before financing a dental practice purchase?

They typically review borrower credit and experience, purchase agreement, practice financials, cash flow, lease or premises control, insurance, corporate documents, security, guarantees and transition risk.

Is 100 percent dental practice financing available in Canada?

Some professional banking pages advertise up to 100 percent financing for eligible healthcare or practice purchases, but terms are conditional. Confirm down payment, amortization, deferral, security, fees and approval criteria directly with lenders.

Should a buyer choose an asset purchase or a share purchase?

That is a legal and tax decision. Asset purchases and share purchases affect liabilities, tax treatment, financing structure and seller expectations differently. Model both with legal, tax and lending advice before signing binding terms.

Is this article financial, tax or legal advice?

No. It is educational acquisition-readiness guidance. Buyers should rely on qualified lenders, lawyers, accountants, insurance advisors and valuation professionals for their specific transaction.
