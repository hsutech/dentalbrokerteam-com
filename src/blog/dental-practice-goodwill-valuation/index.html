Dental Practice Goodwill Valuation: 7 Checks Before You Buy

Dental practice goodwill is worth examining through the earnings and relationships a new owner can reasonably sustain. Before accepting a figure, test what depends on the seller, what belongs to the practice and what the purchase price already includes.

By Dental Broker Team · Published October 16, 2026

What dental practice goodwill actually represents

Dental practice goodwill valuation asks how much of a clinic's value comes from an established business that a purchaser can continue operating. Patient relationships, the team, reputation and working systems can contribute. The decisive question is whether those advantages survive the seller's departure. A busy appointment book alone does not answer it.

For an Ontario buyer reviewing an appraisal, start by separating three questions: what is the whole practice worth, what supports the goodwill within that value, and how will the agreed purchase price be allocated? Those questions connect, but they are not interchangeable. This guide focuses on testing the goodwill assumption before accepting a price. For the broader methodology, see our guide to valuing a dental practice in Ontario.

CBIZ's goodwill explainer describes goodwill as a residual intangible and distinguishes income, market and asset approaches. It also explains why a balance-sheet amount may differ from current economic value. Its US tax discussion and transaction percentages should not be imported into an Ontario purchase.

This is an educational decision framework, not a valuation conclusion or legal, tax or accounting advice. A qualified valuator and your transaction advisors need the actual records, transaction terms and valuation purpose.

Personal goodwill versus practice goodwill

Personal goodwill depends heavily on an individual dentist: patients specifically requesting that clinician, referrals based on a personal relationship, or procedures that only the seller performs. Practice goodwill is supported by the organisation around that person: familiar location, reliable scheduling, a stable team and continuity across providers. A clinic can have both.

Use the distinction as a question to investigate, not an automatic percentage deduction. A seller with a strong reputation may introduce patients successfully to a successor. Conversely, a clinic with an established trading name can still depend on one person's clinical output. The buyer's abilities and the proposed handover matter.

7 checks before you accept a goodwill value

1. Identify exactly what the valuation includes

Read the definition of the interest being valued before looking at the headline number. Does the report describe operating assets, shares, or an ownership interest? Are cash, debt, receivables and real estate included or excluded? Is the equipment already part of the operating value? Ask for a written reconciliation where terminology differs between the appraisal and offer.

A useful review question is: “Show me how the operating value becomes the price I will pay.” If goodwill is listed separately, establish whether it is an allocation within that price or an additional amount. An unclear label can make two otherwise similar offers look very different.

2. Test earnings after replacing the seller's work

A purchaser needs to understand the work behind the earnings. Separate the owner's clinical hours, management duties and exceptional procedures from activity delivered by associates and the hygiene team. Then ask what it would cost to maintain that activity under the proposed ownership model. A buyer who plans fewer clinical days should not silently assume the seller's output will continue.

Grewal Guyatt's Canadian dental valuation guidance explains the use of normalized cash flows and identifies staff, premises and patient characteristics as relevant considerations. Normalization requires evidence; it is not permission to remove inconvenient expenses.

For your own review, make a short schedule of each claimed adjustment, its supporting record and the reason it would change. Ask the valuator to explain which adjustments represent sustainable economics and which depend on a particular purchaser's plans.

3. Reconcile patient activity with the continuity story

A total chart count can include people who have moved, changed dentists or not attended for years. Ask how “active patient” is defined, which reporting date applies and whether comparisons use the same definition. Request explanations for changes in software or reporting settings before treating an apparent increase as growth.

Then examine activity by provider and service category using appropriately controlled information. Does the practice have regular attendance across the team, or is activity concentrated around the departing dentist? How much of the forward schedule consists of confirmed appointments? Separate observed activity from an assumption about future loyalty. Neither a chart nor an appointment guarantees that a patient will remain.

4. Check whether the team and systems can carry the handover

Ask who handles scheduling, recall, billing, supplier relationships and day-to-day decisions. A process may appear well established while relying on knowledge held by one employee. Your review should identify both the documented procedure and the person who can perform it.

Consider a hypothetical clinic with a full hygiene schedule but one coordinator who manages all recall activity informally. The schedule is useful evidence; the absence of a documented process is a separate continuity risk. The response might be training and documentation before closing, rather than assuming either that the goodwill is worthless or that nothing needs to change.

Have employment and associate arrangements reviewed by the appropriate advisors. Do not treat a seller's expectation that everyone will stay as a commitment from those individuals.

5. Verify control of the location and business identity

If the goodwill story depends on convenience, parking, local recognition or proximity to a community, the premises deserve close attention. Ask your lawyer to examine the lease term, renewal rights, assignment or change-of-control provisions, and any relocation or redevelopment clauses. Establish which consents are required and who will obtain them.

Also inventory the practical channels through which patients reach the clinic: telephone numbers, website domain, booking tools and business accounts. Identify ownership, administrator access and any transfer restrictions. A purchaser should know what will be available on the first working day, rather than discovering after closing that an essential account belongs to a former contractor.

For Burlington and other Ontario locations, assess the actual catchment area and premises. A broad regional label does not establish the durability of one practice's goodwill.

6. Turn the transition promise into specific actions

“The seller will help” leaves too much unresolved. Discuss the intended working schedule, patient introductions, completion of ongoing treatment, referral communications and responsibility for unanswered questions. Identify what happens if the seller becomes unavailable sooner than planned. The parties' lawyers should translate the agreed obligations into suitable terms.

The RCDSO ownership-change guidance says the selling dentist must notify patients in writing, addresses confidentiality agreements before disclosure to potential purchasers, and emphasises continuity of care. It also makes clear that commercial arrangements must not restrict patients' choice of dentist or access to records.

Goodwill therefore cannot mean ownership of patients. Keep your commercial assumptions separate from professional responsibilities. Use the transition timeline guide to coordinate the handover once the underlying responsibilities are agreed.

7. Stress-test the assumption and reconcile the allocation

Ask the valuator to show how the conclusion responds if a key assumption changes: lower continuing activity, a different provider mix, additional staffing cost or less seller support. Choose scenarios relevant to the actual clinic. Avoid adding an arbitrary “standard attrition” deduction to every practice.

A sensitivity discussion should make the decision clearer: which unresolved fact could materially change the price, and what evidence would resolve it? It should not disguise a forecast as a guarantee. If the assumed continuity cannot be supported, options include further investigation, revised terms, a different price or deciding not to proceed.

The CRA's guidance on buying an existing business describes reasonable allocation to assets and inventory, with the remaining amount attributed to goodwill. Ask your accountant and lawyer to reconcile the allocation with the agreement. Do not select an unsupported goodwill amount merely to pursue a preferred tax result.

A residual calculation is not proof of market value

Consider a simplified hypothetical asset purchase with an agreed price of CAD 900,000 and CAD 250,000 allocated to all net identifiable assets included in that purchase. The residual would be CAD 650,000. This illustration explains subtraction only; it is not an Ontario benchmark, a practice appraisal or a suggested allocation.

The difficult work comes before the subtraction: establishing that the total price and the identifiable asset values are supportable and use the same transaction scope. A large residual can result from an excessive price just as easily as from valuable goodwill. Ask your advisors to reconcile any separately identified intangible assets, liabilities and exclusions rather than using equipment book value as a shortcut.

Similarly, a percentage of revenue cannot by itself prove goodwill. Two clinics with the same collections may require different staffing, facilities and clinical capabilities. Review broader Canadian valuation multiples only alongside the earnings definition and evidence behind the comparison.

What to request before moving forward

Bring a one-page question list to your appraisal review: transaction scope, earnings adjustments, provider dependence, patient-report definitions, premises continuity and the agreed handover. For each unresolved item, name the person who can answer it and the document required. This keeps a goodwill discussion connected to decisions instead of competing opinions about reputation.

A seller can use the same list to prepare evidence without promising perfect retention. A buyer can use it to distinguish a missing document from a risk that changes the deal. Frequently asked questions

What is goodwill in a dental practice?

It is intangible business value associated with factors such as established patient relationships, reputation and operating continuity. In a purchase allocation, goodwill is a residual after the relevant identifiable assets are valued. Its amount needs support from the transaction and valuation evidence.

Is goodwill always a fixed percentage of revenue?

No. A percentage may be used as a comparison, but it cannot establish the value of a particular clinic. Earnings quality, owner dependence, operating costs and continuity assumptions need to be examined.

Does buying goodwill guarantee that patients stay?

No. Patients retain their choice of dentist. A transition plan can support continuity, but neither the purchase price nor a patient list guarantees future attendance.

Should goodwill be added to an earnings-based valuation?

Not automatically. First establish what that valuation already includes. Adding a separate goodwill amount to a value that already reflects the operating business can double count the same economic benefits.

Who should review the goodwill figure?

A qualified valuator should address the valuation assumptions, with your accountant and lawyer reviewing allocation and transaction terms. Give them the same documents and proposed deal structure so their conclusions can be reconciled.
