Dental Practice Broker Fees Ontario: 7 Cost Checks Before You Sign

Dental practice broker fees in Ontario should be compared by total service scope, buyer reach, valuation support, exclusivity, holdover terms and closing economics, not by one percentage or promise.

By Dental Broker Team - Published September 9, 2026

Direct answer: ask what the fee buys and when it is earned

There is no single standard dental practice broker fee in Ontario that every seller should expect. Public results show several fee models: a percentage of sale price, a flat-fee advisory model, a retainer plus success fee, a separate valuation fee, or a legal-led sale route where brokerage commission is reduced or avoided. The right comparison is not "commission versus no commission." It is whether the arrangement gives the seller enough valuation support, buyer access, confidentiality control and transaction discipline for the decision at hand.

For orientation, the American Dental Association article on dental practice sale costs says business consulting or broker fees are often the largest selling cost and describes broker fees around 10% of practice value in the U.S. market. Copper Dental Transitions publicly lists an 8% practice-sales fee and a separate buyer-representation package. Practice Advocate, an Ontario seller-advocacy firm, describes a flat-rate approach not based on production. DMC LLP argues for a legal-led unbrokered route and criticizes 10% commission models. Those examples are useful because they show the range of structures, but they are not a quote for your Ontario practice.

Ontario dentists should also remember that the sale of a dental practice is not just a marketing assignment. It can involve patient records, confidentiality, lease assignment, employee and associate issues, tax planning, valuation, financing, legal documents and post-closing transition. Dental Broker Team works with Ontario dentists on appraisal and transition planning, but any engagement should spell out fees, services and limits before the seller signs.

7 cost checks before signing a dental practice broker agreement

1. Identify the fee model, not just the headline number

Start by asking exactly how the provider gets paid. A percentage commission can be simple if it is payable only on closing, but it may be expensive on a larger practice. A flat fee can be predictable, but the seller must confirm what work is included. A retainer plus success fee can align some effort before launch, but it may leave the seller paying even if no deal closes. A free valuation can be helpful for an initial conversation, but it may be tied to a later engagement.

Ask for the fee method in writing: percentage of what, flat amount for what, retainer credited how, success fee earned when, and whether HST or other taxes are extra. If real estate is part of the transaction, clarify whether the fee applies to the practice, the premises or both.

2. Compare seller-side and buyer-side compensation

Many dental practice sales are marketed from the seller side, meaning the seller pays the listing or sale advisor at closing. That does not mean buyer-side fees never exist. Copper Dental Transitions, for example, publicly lists separate buyer representation pricing. RECO's remuneration-clause bulletin explains that Ontario representation agreements should state what a buyer client must pay and how that amount may change if a seller offers to compensate buyer brokerage fees.

The seller should understand whether any buyer-side compensation, co-operating brokerage amount, referral fee or multiple-representation arrangement may affect the deal. A fee can be legally documented and still change the incentives around advice. If the same firm is involved with both sides, ask how conflicts and reduced advocacy will be handled.

3. Check whether valuation is included, independent or credited

Broker fee conversations often blur into valuation fee conversations. A broker may include a broker opinion of value, charge separately for a valuation, credit an upfront fee against the final commission, or recommend a separate independent valuator. None of those structures is automatically wrong. The issue is whether the valuation is fit for the decision.

If the valuation will anchor the asking price, buyer financing and negotiation strategy, ask what records will be reviewed, whether earnings will be normalized, how patient and hygiene data will be considered and whether the report has reliance limits. For a deeper look at report scope, see the guide to dental practice appraisal cost in Ontario.

4. Read exclusivity and holdover clauses carefully

Brokerage and advisory agreements can include exclusivity periods, cancellation rules and holdover clauses. A holdover can require payment if a buyer introduced during the engagement later purchases the practice after the agreement ends. That may be reasonable when the advisor created the buyer opportunity, but the period and trigger should be clear.

DMC LLP's unbrokered article specifically criticizes long listing terms and holdover exposure. RECO's representation-agreement bulletin says agreements should identify expiry, remuneration method, services, termination provisions and circumstances where remuneration may change. Even when the provider is dental-specialized and personable, the contract terms matter.

5. Separate marketing cost from buyer qualification

A lower fee is not useful if it only buys a listing and no real buyer screening. A higher fee is not justified by vague promises of a network unless the provider explains how buyers are identified, qualified and moved through diligence. Ask whether the fee covers confidential buyer outreach, NDA management, information memoranda, buyer financing checks, offer comparison, data-room support and post-LOI diligence.

Ontario practice sales can involve sensitive patient and business information. The RCDSO change-of-practice-ownership FAQ explains that personal health information can be disclosed to a potential purchaser for assessment only after a confidentiality agreement is in place. Broker fees should be evaluated partly by how well the provider protects that staged disclosure process.

6. Compare commission savings against outcome risk

A flat-fee or unbrokered route can save money when the seller already has a buyer, a defensible valuation and strong legal and accounting support. Practice Advocate's Ontario page argues that flat fees can avoid tying cost to production. DMC LLP argues that legal-led sale work can replace traditional brokerage in some cases. Those alternatives may be attractive, especially when the seller has a known successor or wants targeted support instead of a full open-market process.

The risk is underestimating the value of competition and process control. If one buyer falls apart after months of diligence, the apparent fee savings may disappear. If a seller accepts a weak offer because the practice was never properly exposed to qualified buyers, the lost value may exceed the commission saved. Compare likely net proceeds, certainty, confidentiality and workload, not just invoice amount.

7. Confirm which professional roles are outside the fee

A dental practice broker fee usually does not replace legal, tax, accounting or lender advice. Delta Law's Ontario dental sale guide points to asset versus share structure, lease review, representations and warranties, restrictive covenants and transition periods as legal considerations. The Canada Revenue Agency's selling-a-business guidance also shows that tax and account registration issues can arise when a business is sold.

Ask the broker or advisor to state what is not included. Who drafts or reviews the purchase agreement? Who advises on tax structure? Who handles employment or associate agreements? Who reviews lease assignment? Who speaks with the lender? A clear boundary protects the seller from paying a broker fee and then discovering that critical parts of the transaction still need separate paid support.

Percentage success fee The seller wants a broker-led market process with buyer reach, screening and closing momentum. Percentage of which sale value, payable when, and does it include valuation, marketing, buyer qualification and diligence support?
Flat-fee seller advisory The seller wants defined support without tying fee size to production or purchase price. What happens if the sale takes longer, requires more buyers, or needs negotiation beyond the fixed scope?

Questions to ask before you sign

Use a short written checklist before committing: What exact services are included? Is the fee a percentage, flat amount, retainer, success fee or combination? Is valuation included? Are marketing expenses separate? Is there exclusivity? How long is the term? Is there a holdover period? What buyer introductions trigger payment? What happens if you already know the buyer? Is buyer-side compensation involved? Who handles legal documents, tax planning and lease assignment? What information will be shared before and after an NDA?

If the provider cannot answer those questions plainly, pause before signing. A professional process should make the economics easier to understand, not harder. How to think about value, not just price

The lowest fee can be expensive if it produces weak buyer competition, poor confidentiality, a failed transaction or an unsupported price. The highest fee can also be hard to justify if it only buys generic listing exposure and vague advice. The useful question is what the seller is buying: valuation judgment, a qualified buyer pool, quiet outreach, NDA discipline, offer comparison, diligence support, negotiation sequencing and transition planning.

For Ontario owners, start with the outcome you need. A near-retirement seller with no successor may need a managed market. A principal selling to an associate may need valuation, legal and tax support more than broad marketing. A practice with real estate, multiple associates or DSO interest may need a more structured advisory team. The fee should match that file, and it should leave the seller clear on next steps before any confidential information leaves the practice.

FAQ

How much are dental practice broker fees in Ontario?

There is no single public Ontario fee. Fees can be commission-based, flat fee, retainer plus success fee, or bundled with valuation and sale-preparation services. Ask each provider for a written fee schedule and scope.

Are dental broker fees fixed by a regulator?

No. RECO guidance says agents must not suggest remuneration is fixed or approved by RECO, a government authority, a real estate board or an association. The method and circumstances for payment should be documented in the agreement.

Can I avoid broker commission if I already have a buyer?

Sometimes, but do not skip valuation, confidentiality, legal documents, tax planning, financing checks or transition planning. A known buyer reduces the need for broad marketing, not the need for a disciplined sale process.

Should broker fees include a valuation?

They may include a broker opinion of value, but a broker-led appraisal is not always the same as an independent valuation report. Confirm scope, records reviewed, reliance limits and whether any upfront valuation fee is credited later.

What is a holdover clause?

A holdover clause can require compensation if a buyer introduced during the agreement later completes the purchase after the agreement ends. Ask which buyers are covered, how long the holdover lasts and what payment is triggered.

Is a flat-fee sale model better than commission?

It depends. Flat fees can create predictability and may reduce cost in larger transactions, but the seller should verify buyer reach, negotiation support, confidentiality controls and what happens if the sale becomes more complex.
