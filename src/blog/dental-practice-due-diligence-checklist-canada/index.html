Dental Practice Due Diligence Checklist Canada: 10 Buyer Checks Before Closing

A Canadian dental practice due diligence checklist should test the facts behind the purchase price, cash flow, patient base, lease, staff, equipment, privacy controls and transition plan before a buyer removes conditions.

By Dental Broker Team - Published September 16, 2026

Direct answer: verify the deal story before you remove conditions

Dental practice due diligence in Canada is the process of testing whether the practice you plan to buy matches the story you were told. The checklist should connect price, earnings, patient flow, hygiene strength, lease security, staffing, equipment, regulatory responsibilities, financing and transition risk. Receiving a file is not enough. Each important assumption should have evidence, an owner and a decision.

This article is for buyers who are already reviewing or preparing to review a specific opportunity. If you are earlier in the search, start with the first-time buyer guide. If the financing file is your main blocker, use the guide on dental practice financing in Canada. If you are comparing listings, the guide to Ontario dental practices for sale explains how to read public listing signals before signing an NDA.

10 dental practice due diligence checks for Canadian buyers

1. Reconcile the price to verified earnings

Start with the proposed price, not a generic checklist. Ask what earnings number the price depends on and how that number was built. Review tax returns, profit and loss statements, year-to-date results, production and collection reports, payroll, lab, supplies, rent, owner compensation and add-backs. An adjustment is not credible just because it appears on a seller schedule. Ask whether it is recurring, transferable and supportable after closing.

Look at monthly trends, not only annual totals. A practice with flat annual collections can still have recent decline. A practice with growth can still depend on one associate, one marketing channel or one procedure category. The question is not whether the practice ever produced a good year. The question is whether the cash flow you are financing is durable enough for your plan.

2. Test patient base and hygiene quality

Patient count can be defined many ways. Ask how active patients are counted, what date range is used, how many new patients arrived in the last 12 to 24 months and how hygiene recall is performing. Review production by provider, hygiene production, cancellations, no-shows, unscheduled treatment, payer mix and referral patterns where available.

Be careful with patient-level information. In Ontario, RCDSO guidance says personal health information may be disclosed to a potential purchaser for assessment only after a confidentiality agreement is in place. In many cases, aggregated or anonymized reports can answer early questions before identifiable records are needed.

3. Review clinical mix and owner dependency

A buyer should understand which procedures drive revenue and who performs them. If the seller generates most high-value treatment, ask whether you can clinically and ethically continue that mix. If associates produce a large share, review agreements, retention risk and compensation. If the practice refers out significant work, decide whether that is an opportunity or a sign that the current patient base expects care elsewhere.

Owner dependency is not automatically bad. It becomes a risk when the purchase price assumes revenue you cannot reasonably replace. Tie transition promises to actual workback terms, patient communication and your own clinical fit.

4. Audit the lease, premises and location risk

For a dental practice, the lease can be as important as the equipment. Review remaining term, renewal options, assignment rights, landlord consent, rent escalation, permitted use, exclusivity, demolition or relocation language, signage, parking, accessibility and maintenance obligations. A lender may be comfortable with the practice only if the location can be controlled for long enough to support the loan.

If the seller owns the building, separate the practice purchase from any real estate conversation. If relocation is possible, model cost, downtime, patient retention and regulatory requirements. Goodwill is easier to finance when the premises risk is understood.

5. Inspect equipment, technology and systems

Create an equipment list with age, condition, ownership, leases, liens, service history and expected replacement needs. Include dental units, chairs, imaging, sterilization, compressors, suction, computers, server or cloud systems, practice-management software and cybersecurity basics. Confirm which assets are included in the purchase and which contracts must be assigned.

Equipment age does not tell the whole story. A well-maintained older operatory can be workable. A newer system with missing service records, licence issues or integration problems can create expense. Budget near-term capital separately from purchase price.

6. Read staff, associate and contractor obligations

Ask for staff roles, tenure, wages, benefits, vacation, payroll practices, policy manuals and employment agreements where available. Review associate and contractor agreements, including compensation, notice, restrictive covenants, hygiene arrangements and control of schedules. Employment and associate issues can affect continuity immediately after closing.

Staff culture also matters. The CDA buyer FAQ highlights practice culture, team relationships and leadership style as part of evaluating a practice. A clean spreadsheet does not fix a team that is likely to leave, and a good team can make a transition much smoother.

7. Check legal structure and assumed obligations

Asset purchases and share purchases expose buyers to different obligations. Delta Law's Ontario guide explains that a share purchase can bring ownership of the existing corporation with its assets and liabilities, while an asset purchase usually involves selected assets. Your lawyer and accountant should connect structure to liabilities, tax, contracts, patient records, employees, leases and financing.

Review corporate records, supplier contracts, service agreements, equipment financing, insurance, litigation or threatened claims, permits, professional corporation steps and purchase-agreement conditions. If something cannot be assigned, determine whether closing can happen at all.

8. Protect privacy and patient-record responsibilities

Dental practice diligence is not ordinary customer-list diligence. Patient records involve professional and privacy obligations. In Ontario, RCDSO guidance says the selling dentist is responsible for transfer of patient records to another health information custodian, and the seller must notify patients of a change in ownership in writing. The purchase agreement may also address access to records after closing for complaint or claim defence.

Use staged disclosure and a request log. Record what was requested, why it was needed, who reviewed it and whether the answer changed the deal. Sensitive records should not float around a general data room without controls.

9. Tie financing conditions to diligence findings

Financing and diligence should move together. A lender may ask for financial statements, purchase agreement, lease, valuation support, insurance, borrower information, security documents and corporate details. If diligence reveals a short lease, falling collections, equipment replacement or staffing risk, update the financing model before removing conditions.

The new article on building a dental practice acquisition financing file explains how buyer, practice and transaction information need to reconcile. A strong due diligence process gives that financing file better evidence.

10. Convert findings into a closing or renegotiation decision

Every major finding should lead to a decision. Proceed, renegotiate, add a condition, require a holdback, change transition terms, ask for more evidence or walk away. BDC's guidance is blunt about this point: due diligence can confirm the investment case, but it can also reveal information that changes the buyer's perception of the deal.

Do not remove conditions just because everyone is tired. Closing transfers the problem set to you. A disciplined buyer leaves due diligence with resolved questions, documented assumptions, advisor sign-off and a transition plan for the first months of ownership.

Red flags that deserve a pause

Pause if numbers do not reconcile, if seller explanations change, if active patient counts are vague, if the lease cannot be assigned, if equipment ownership is unclear, if staff agreements are missing, if the seller resists reasonable advisor review, if privacy controls are weak or if financing depends on optimistic growth. None of these automatically kills a deal. Each one needs a documented answer before closing.

Also pause if you feel yourself rationalizing negative findings because the practice looks attractive. A dental acquisition can be a career-shaping move. It deserves an evidence standard that survives excitement.

How to run the checklist without slowing the deal unnecessarily

Set up a diligence tracker with request, source, reviewer, due date, answer, open issue and decision. Give your lawyer, accountant, lender and advisor clear responsibilities. Start with the items most likely to change price or closing certainty: earnings, lease, records, staff obligations, financing and structure. Lower-risk housekeeping can follow once the major issues are moving.

The practical goal is not a perfect file. It is a clear decision. A well-run checklist should tell you whether to proceed, renegotiate, ask for more protection or step away. That discipline protects the buyer, respects the seller's time and improves the chance that the first year of ownership starts from facts rather than surprises.

FAQ

What should a dental practice due diligence checklist include in Canada?

It should include financial statements, tax returns, production and collection reports, active patient and hygiene data, lease terms, staff and associate agreements, equipment condition, contracts, privacy controls, regulatory issues, financing conditions and the post-closing transition plan.

Is due diligence the same as a dental practice valuation?

No. A valuation supports price and value assumptions. Due diligence tests whether the underlying facts support the purchase decision. Both can inform each other, but they answer different questions.

When should a buyer start due diligence?

Do preliminary review before the letter of intent, then complete formal diligence after confidentiality, access and timelines are agreed. Do not remove conditions until key questions are resolved.

Can buyers review patient records during due diligence?

Patient information requires privacy controls. In Ontario, RCDSO guidance says disclosure to a potential purchaser for assessment requires a confidentiality agreement first. Confirm provincial rules before identifiable patient information is shared.

Is this legal, tax, privacy or lending advice?

No. It is educational acquisition guidance. Buyers should rely on qualified Canadian legal, tax, accounting, privacy, lending, valuation and clinical advisors for their specific transaction.
