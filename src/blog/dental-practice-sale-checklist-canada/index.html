Dental Practice Sale Checklist Canada: 10 Steps Before You List

A Canadian dental practice sale checklist should organize the value story, buyer file, privacy controls, lease, staff documents, tax questions and closing plan before the practice is exposed to the market.

Direct answer: prepare the sale file before you prepare the listing

The practical checklist for selling a dental practice in Canada is not just a pile of financial statements. It is a staged file that explains what is being sold, why the number is credible, which risks are controlled, how patient information will be protected and what must happen between offer and closing. A buyer, lender, lawyer, accountant and transition advisor will each look at the practice from a different angle. The seller's job is to make those questions answerable without exposing sensitive information too early.

This topic overlaps with general transition planning, but the intent is more concrete. The existing dental practice transition checklist explains the broad handoff mindset. This guide focuses on the sale file itself: documents, diligence, valuation support, confidentiality gates and closing decisions for Canadian sellers. If you are still deciding which route to use, compare this with the guide on the best way to sell a dental practice in Ontario.

The Dental Broker Team works with Ontario dentists on appraisals, sale preparation and buyer conversations. Treat this article as educational guidance, not legal, tax, accounting or valuation advice. Provincial college requirements and deal structure can change the right sequence.

10-step dental practice sale checklist for Canadian sellers

1. Define the sale goal and likely buyer type

Start with the decision, not the listing. Are you retiring fully, reducing clinical hours, selling to an associate, testing DSO interest, bringing in a partner or preparing for a future sale? The answer changes timing, confidentiality, buyer outreach, price expectations and post-closing obligations. A known associate buyer may need valuation and legal structure more than broad marketing. An open-market sale may need buyer qualification, a confidential information memorandum and a stronger process for comparing offers.

2. Get a defensible valuation baseline

A checklist without a valuation framework can produce organized paperwork but weak pricing. Before buyers shape the narrative, assemble the inputs behind value: normalized earnings, production by provider, hygiene strength, active patient trends, procedure mix, lease stability, equipment condition, staffing, growth opportunities and transition assumptions. If the number must be relied on by partners, lenders, tax planners or a dispute process, ask whether a formal independent valuation is required. The guide on how to value a dental practice in Ontario explains the difference between a quick market view and a more supportable valuation process.

3. Organize three to five years of financial records

Most buyers and lenders want a trend, not a snapshot. Prepare profit and loss statements, tax returns, balance sheets if available, production and collections reports, payroll summaries, accounts receivable aging, owner adjustments and notes on one-time expenses. Seller-side guides often reference three to five years because a buyer wants to see whether revenue, collections, expenses and profitability are stable. Do not wait until an offer is signed to reconcile obvious gaps.

4. Build the practice performance file

Financial statements rarely explain a dental practice by themselves. Add reports for active patients, new patients, hygiene recall, provider production, procedure mix, cancellations, unscheduled treatment, referral patterns and hours of operation. If performance has softened, explain why. If there is upside, support it with facts rather than optimism. Buyers pay for clarity and transferability, not just gross production.

5. Review the lease, premises and equipment

The lease can create or destroy lender confidence. Review term, renewal options, assignment rules, demolition or relocation rights, permitted use, rent escalations and landlord consent requirements. If the seller owns the premises, decide whether the real estate is included, leased separately or excluded. For equipment, list owned items, leased items, financing obligations, service contracts, age and condition. A buyer cannot treat leased equipment as owned assets unless the obligations are understood.

6. Clean up staff, associate and contractor documents

Buyers will ask who works in the practice, what each person does, how compensation works and whether agreements can be assigned or continued. Organize staff lists, wage information, benefits, vacation obligations, policy manuals, associate agreements, hygienist or contractor arrangements and any non-solicit or non-compete language that should be reviewed by counsel. Staff communication should usually wait until the right stage, but the documents should be ready before diligence begins.

7. Protect patient records and confidential information

Patient information is not a normal customer list. In Ontario, RCDSO guidance says personal health information can be disclosed to a potential purchaser for assessment only after a confidentiality agreement is in place. The seller also has patient-notification responsibilities after a change in ownership. Other provinces have their own regulators and privacy frameworks, so confirm local rules before sharing identifiable or sensitive records.

Use staged disclosure. Early buyer materials can summarize practice profile, geography, operatories, revenue range and high-level fit. Detailed financials, patient data, chart samples or clinical diligence should wait until qualification and confidentiality protections are in place.

8. Decide who will manage buyer screening and offers

A sale checklist should include buyer process, not just seller documents. Decide how buyers will be screened for licensure, financing capacity, clinical fit, timing, confidentiality and seriousness. Clarify who handles NDAs, buyer questions, information requests, offer comparison and deal momentum. If you are comparing broker-led, private or advisor-led routes, the guide to selling with or without a broker can help frame the tradeoffs.

9. Flag tax, structure and closing questions early

Canadian dental practice sales can be structured as asset sales, share sales or more complex arrangements depending on the corporation, assets, liabilities, buyer preferences and tax planning. CRA guidance highlights business number, payroll, GST/HST, asset allocation, goodwill and capital gains considerations. Those points should be discussed with qualified advisors before the letter of intent hardens into a purchase agreement.

10. Plan the patient, staff and clinical transition

The closing date is not the end of the checklist. The buyer and seller still need a handoff plan for patients, staff, ongoing treatment, retreatment questions, receivables, deposits, records access, signs, stationery, software permissions and seller workback. RCDSO guidance emphasizes continuity of patient care and planning around unfinished treatment. A stronger transition plan can reduce buyer anxiety and protect the seller's legacy after closing.

What not to do before going to market

Do not share detailed records with an unqualified buyer. Do not promise a valuation number without explaining the assumptions. Do not sign a long engagement, lease amendment, equipment financing agreement or associate arrangement without considering sale impact. Do not tell staff too early without a communication plan. Do not assume the buyer's lawyer, lender or accountant will clean up seller-side gaps for you.

Also avoid over-polishing weak facts. If hygiene has declined, if the lease is short, if owner dependency is high or if financial reporting has gaps, address the issue directly. A transparent file can still sell. A surprise in diligence can cost time, leverage and trust.

How to use this checklist with advisors

Bring the checklist to your broker, lawyer, accountant, valuator and lender conversation. Ask each advisor which items affect price, tax, closing certainty, confidentiality and timing. Then sequence the work. Some issues need months, such as lease negotiations or revenue trend improvement. Others need clean documentation, such as equipment lists and payroll summaries. Dental Broker Team's appraisal work can help sellers organize the value story before buyer conversations start, and the Sell Your Dental Practice page outlines the broader seller path.

The best checklist is not the longest one. It is the one that turns a private, high-stakes decision into a controlled process. Prepare the evidence, protect the information, involve the right advisors and only then decide how the practice should be introduced to buyers.

FAQ

What should be in a dental practice sale checklist in Canada?

Include valuation purpose, financial records, production and hygiene reports, patient record responsibilities, lease terms, staff and associate agreements, equipment and liabilities, buyer screening, tax structure, closing tasks and post-sale transition.

When should a Canadian dentist start preparing to sell?

Many sellers benefit from preparing 12 to 24 months before a sale, especially if financial records, lease terms, staff documents or patient retention trends need improvement. Shorter timelines are possible when the file is already clean.

Can patient records be shown to a potential buyer?

In Ontario, RCDSO guidance says personal health information may be disclosed for purchaser assessment only after a confidentiality agreement is in place. Dentists should confirm provincial rules before sharing patient information.

Do I need a broker to use this checklist?

No. The checklist is useful for broker-led, private and advisor-led sales. The support model changes who manages buyer outreach, qualification and diligence, but the seller still needs an organized sale file.

Is this legal, tax or valuation advice?

No. It is educational preparation guidance. Sellers should rely on qualified legal, tax, accounting, valuation and transaction advisors for their specific practice and province.
