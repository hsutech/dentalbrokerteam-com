Retiring Dentist Succession Planning in Ontario: 7 Decisions

Choose a successor and an exit route that fit your intended retirement. Start with ownership, clinical work and financial needs, then document a fallback.

By Dental Broker Team · Published October 30, 2026

ContentsWhere to startCompare succession routes7 retirement decisionsYour advisor meetingFAQs

What should a retiring Ontario dentist decide first?

Start by separating three decisions: when to transfer ownership, when to stop treating patients, and how to fund life after practice income ends. Those dates can differ. A succession plan should identify a preferred successor, the conditions that make the transfer workable, and a fallback if your health or the buyer's plans change.

For an owner in Burlington or elsewhere in Ontario, the best route depends on more than the offer price. A proposal that requires years of clinical work may conflict with your retirement goal. An associate who knows the patients may still need financing and a workable ownership agreement. A planned closure needs its own budget and arrangements for continuity.

This guide compares those choices before you engage advisors or negotiate a sale. The seven-decision framework is an editorial planning aid, not a ranking of buyers. It is educational information, not individual legal, tax or investment advice.

Compare the main succession routes

BDC’s business exit guide distinguishes succession, an outside sale and shutting down. For a dental practice, use those broad routes as a starting point, then examine the actual terms and professional requirements. The identity of the buyer alone cannot tell you how soon you will be free of ownership or clinical commitments.

These choices can overlap: an associate purchase can be staged, and an external sale can include a short clinical transition. Treat the table as a shortlist for discussion, not evidence that every route is available to every practice. Ask counsel to review eligibility and the proposed ownership structure.

7 decisions before choosing your successor

1. Define the work you are prepared to do after selling

Write down the latest date you want to finish clinical work, your preferred weekly schedule beforehand, and the management tasks you want to stop. Include holidays, physical limitations and family commitments. A vague willingness to “help with the transition” leaves too much room for different expectations.

When reviewing a proposal, translate its conditions into an ordinary working week. Who controls your appointment book? Can you reduce days? What happens if you cannot maintain the proposed schedule? Ask your lawyer to review termination terms and the consequences of an earlier departure. Keep clinical compensation separate from the price paid for the practice.

For example, an owner who wants to stop practising next spring should question an offer tied to several further years of production. That is a hypothetical fit problem, not a prediction about any buyer. Identify the mismatch before treating the headline amount as retirement capital.

2. Establish what the sale must contribute to retirement

Bring your household spending plan, debts, investments and expected non-practice income to a qualified financial planner. Ask for scenarios showing an earlier exit, a delayed sale and lower net proceeds. The useful output is an explanation of what changes in each scenario, including whether further clinical income is needed.

Siskinds' dental succession guidance highlights that the destination of sale proceeds, including whether they are received personally or through a corporation, affects retirement planning. Have your accountant show how the proposed transaction connects to money available for personal spending.

Keep estimated sale value, debt repayment, professional costs, tax assumptions and any deferred payment separate. Do not treat a conditional future amount as cash available at closing. Ask what evidence supports each assumption and when the calculation needs refreshing. The existing practice sale tax considerations guide provides questions for the tax discussion without turning this succession plan into a tax calculation.

3. Test successor readiness before making promises

An interested associate is a candidate, not a completed succession plan. Establish whether they actually want ownership, what responsibilities they expect to take on, and whether they have begun a credible financing conversation. Agree on a process for discussing value before either party becomes attached to an informal price.

Use separate professional advice where interests differ. For a staged transaction, explore voting rights, distributions, future purchase terms and what happens if one party wants to leave. The associate buy-in comparison covers that ownership choice in more detail.

An outside purchaser also needs a fit assessment. Discuss the clinical services they expect to provide, their approach to introducing changes, and their proposed role for the existing team. Avoid promising patients or staff an outcome that is still being negotiated. Record what has been agreed and what remains an assumption.

4. Decide which continuing obligations you can accept

Retirement can remain complicated after a sale if payments, guarantees or management duties continue. Ask counsel to identify every obligation that survives closing and who can release it. Include personal guarantees, lease commitments, restrictive terms, consulting duties and access to information needed for later payment calculations.

For any deferred consideration, ask your advisors to explain the payment trigger, who controls the relevant decisions, the information you can inspect and the remedy if payment is disputed. Compare proposals on terms you understand rather than assigning the same value to immediate cash and uncertain later receipts.

If a group or DSO proposes retained equity or a continued role, request a clear account of ownership, control and exit rights. Do not assume that a larger organization offers a quicker retirement, or that a familiar individual buyer removes contractual risk. The documents determine the commitment.

5. Separate professional registration from the ownership handover

A practice sale and resignation from professional registration are different events. The RCDSO renewal FAQs state that licences cannot be placed on hold and there is no retired class. Dentists who maintain registration while retired still have fee and quality-assurance obligations.

Confirm the right registration arrangements before agreeing to post-sale clinical work. Ask the College about your intended final clinical date and resignation process, and ask your lawyer how the change affects any professional corporation. Do not infer that a buyer's proposed closing date is also the correct date to resign.

Build a short responsibility list with the lawyer, accountant and College contacts who will answer each question. Obtain current confirmation for your circumstances, especially if ownership changes, continuing work and retirement fall in different calendar years. A calendar reminder is useful only when someone owns the underlying task.

6. Assign responsibility for patients and records

The RCDSO ownership and retirement FAQs explain the selling dentist's responsibilities for transferring records and notifying patients in writing. They also address unfinished treatment and the need to plan for records succession if the custodian becomes incapacitated or dies.

Ask your advisors to translate those duties into a documented handover: who will communicate, who will handle access requests, and how ongoing care will be coordinated. If the practice closes, confirm arrangements for secure retention and patient access. Do not treat possession of the office keys as proof that records responsibilities have been resolved.

Discuss practical access with the software provider and the designated records custodian. A plan should account for the systems actually used, including archived information. Verify the arrangements with the relevant professionals rather than circulating patient information during informal buyer conversations.

7. Choose a fallback that works without your daily involvement

Ask what happens if the preferred successor withdraws, a transaction takes longer than expected, or you must stop working unexpectedly. Identify the person who would coordinate the response and where they would find the essential advisor contacts, agreements and instructions. Review their authority with your lawyer.

A fallback should be operationally plausible. Continuing to own the clinic while recruiting another dentist requires different resources from winding down. Ask your accountant to compare the cash needed to keep operating with the cost of an orderly closure. Include costs that continue even when you reduce clinical days.

Set review points around real changes: a potential buyer's financing decision, a major lease event, a change in health or a change in your desired retirement date. If the original route stops meeting your needs, reopen the decision. Having invested time in one possibility does not make its unresolved conditions disappear.

What to bring to the first succession planning meeting

Prepare a one-page brief before requesting proposals. Include your preferred ownership transfer date, final clinical date, acceptable continuing duties, preferred successor, estimated retirement funding need and fallback. Mark estimates clearly. Bring a list of the documents your advisors need rather than sharing sensitive records indiscriminately.

Ask each professional for a concrete output. The valuator can assess the practice within an agreed scope; the accountant can examine transaction assumptions; the lawyer can review rights and obligations; and the financial planner can connect the expected outcome to household needs. Agree who will reconcile conflicting assumptions.

Frequently asked questions

When should an Ontario dentist start succession planning?

Start while you still have choices about work, ownership and successors. The preparation required depends on your circumstances. Ask advisors which issues require advance work rather than relying on a universal number of months.

Can I sell my practice and continue working?

That can be negotiated, subject to appropriate registration and an agreement defining your role. Review schedule, compensation, duration and termination terms before treating continued work as part of your retirement plan.

Is selling to an associate always the best option?

No. Familiarity is useful, but willingness, financing, ownership terms and your desired exit must align. Compare the proposed arrangement with realistic alternatives and document what happens if the associate does not proceed.

Does selling the practice automatically mean I should resign?

No. Confirm your registration arrangements with the RCDSO in light of any continuing clinical work. Ask your lawyer to coordinate the ownership and corporate implications with the intended retirement date.

What if I have no successor?

Discuss an external sale, a period of continued operation with appropriate support, and an orderly closure with your advisors. Compare feasibility and costs, and establish a specific plan for patients and records.
