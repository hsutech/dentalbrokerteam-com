Questions to Ask a Dental Practice Broker: 10 Before You Sign

The best questions to ask a dental practice broker clarify representation, fees, valuation method, confidentiality, buyer quality, marketing controls, advisor boundaries and what happens if the transaction stalls.

By Dental Broker Team - Published September 21, 2026

Direct answer: interview the broker before the broker interviews your practice

Ask a dental practice broker who they represent, how they are paid, how they value practices, how they protect confidentiality, how they qualify buyers or opportunities and exactly what services are included in the engagement. The goal is not to catch someone out. The goal is to know whether the broker's incentives, process and experience match your transaction before you give them control over one of the most sensitive business decisions of your career.

This guide is for Ontario dentists who are close to a brokerage conversation: selling, buying, evaluating a known associate transition or comparing advisor options. If you are still deciding whether to hire a broker at all, start with the guide to selling a dental practice with or without a broker. If cost is the main concern, read the separate guide to dental practice broker fees in Ontario.

Dental Broker Team works with Ontario dentists on practice appraisals, buyer conversations and transition planning. This article is educational, not legal, tax, accounting, lending, privacy or regulatory advice. Have your own lawyer, accountant and other advisors review the actual engagement and transaction documents.

10 questions to ask a dental practice broker before you sign

1. Who do you represent in this transaction?

Start here because every other answer depends on it. Ask whether the broker represents the seller, the buyer, both parties in different capacities, the transaction itself or a brokerage entity with more than one advisor involved. If you are a seller, ask whether the broker ever represents buyers who may bid on your practice. If you are a buyer, ask whether the broker's first duty is to the seller, the buyer or a separate agreement.

A strong answer names the client relationship clearly and explains conflicts before you have to press. In Ontario real estate contexts, RECO distinguishes brokerage representation, designated representation and multiple representation. Dental transactions do not all follow the same form, so the practical question is simple: whose interests are being promoted, who has access to confidential information and when do disclosures or consents become necessary?

2. How are you paid, and when is the fee earned?

Ask for the complete fee picture in writing: success fee, minimum fee, retainer, valuation fee, marketing charges, reimbursable expenses, taxes, referral fees and payment timing. Also ask what happens if you decline an offer, sell to a known associate, pause the process, terminate the agreement or close after the agreement ends.

Fee transparency is not only about the percentage. A lower visible commission can become expensive if the agreement has unclear minimums, long holdover periods or reimbursable expenses. A higher fee may be reasonable when scope, preparation, buyer screening and diligence support are real. Compare the actual work, not just the headline.

3. What exactly is included in your service?

Ask the broker to walk through the deliverables. Does the engagement include valuation, normalization of financials, confidential marketing materials, buyer qualification, NDA management, showings, offer comparison, advisor coordination, diligence support and transition planning? Which items are included, optional, outsourced or billed separately?

RECO's consumer guidance for brokerage agreements is useful here: services should be clear before the client signs. Do not assume a task is included because it sounds normal. If you expect weekly updates, confidential buyer tracking, lender conversations or help organizing the seller file, ask whether those services are part of the written engagement.

4. How do you value a dental practice before taking it to market?

A broker does not need to reveal a secret formula. They should be able to explain the valuation process in plain English. Ask what financial statements, production reports, hygiene metrics, active-patient definitions, owner adjustments, lease terms, equipment needs, associate dependency and transition assumptions they review before suggesting a price or range.

This question helps separate pricing from positioning. A practice can be marketed at an attractive number without a defensible explanation. For a deeper framework, compare the broker's answer with the guide on how to value a dental practice in Ontario and the article on dental practice valuation multiples in Canada.

5. How will confidentiality be protected from first call to closing?

Ask when the broker reveals the practice name, address, staff details, financials, patient-related information and seller motivation. Ask who signs confidentiality agreements, how buyer access is staged, whether blind profiles are used, how records are stored and what happens if a buyer or advisor breaches confidentiality.

Confidentiality is not a courtesy. It protects staff stability, patient trust, lender conversations and seller negotiating power. In Ontario, RCDSO guidance also places patient-record disclosure within a privacy framework. Early buyer conversations should rely on staged, controlled information, with identifiable personal health information handled only when the proper confidentiality and professional advice are in place.

6. Where do your buyers or opportunities come from?

Sellers should ask how the broker builds the buyer pool. Are candidates individual dentists, associates, local owners, groups, DSOs, private networks or public listing inquiries? How are buyers qualified for financing, clinical fit, timeline and seriousness before they see deeper information?

Buyers should ask a mirrored version: how does the broker source opportunities, and can they show opportunities beyond public listings? If the broker only controls a small inventory, the buyer may still need a broader search plan through colleagues, lenders, suppliers, provincial association resources and other transition advisors. The Canadian Dental Association notes that practices can change hands through several channels, including brokers, associations, colleagues and professional networks.

7. What local dental-transition experience can you verify?

Ask for examples that match your situation without requiring the broker to reveal confidential client details. A rural hygiene-heavy solo practice, a GTA associate buy-in, a multi-provider clinic and a DSO negotiation are not the same assignment. The broker's experience should match the practice type, geography, buyer pool and transition risks.

Do not rely only on total years in business or national deal counts. Ask what made similar deals succeed or stall, what diligence questions appeared, how staff and patient communication were handled and where valuation assumptions changed during negotiation.

8. What contract terms should I review with my lawyer before signing?

Ask the broker to identify the terms that matter, then have your lawyer review them independently. Common terms include scope, exclusivity, term length, cancellation, minimum fee, holdover period, reimbursed expenses, known-buyer treatment, conflict disclosures, privacy obligations and what happens if the transaction does not close.

Be cautious if anyone suggests waiting to involve counsel until late in the process. A dental practice broker can help locate, prepare and coordinate a transaction, but legal documents and rights should be reviewed by a lawyer who represents you. The question is not whether the broker is helpful. The question is whether each advisor stays in their lane.

9. How will offers be compared beyond price?

Ask how the broker helps compare offers when the highest number is not necessarily the strongest result. Terms can differ on financing certainty, deposit, conditions, lease assignment, asset versus share structure, seller transition, staff treatment, closing date, adjustments, non-compete terms, patient communication and post-closing obligations.

A good offer process makes tradeoffs visible. It should not pressure you to accept a headline price while ignoring conditions or buyer capability. Sellers should ask what evidence the broker requires before calling a buyer qualified. Buyers should ask how the broker presents their offer fairly without weakening their negotiating position.

10. What will communication look like if the process gets difficult?

Ask how often you will hear from the broker, what metrics or updates they provide, who your day-to-day contact is and how problems are escalated. Every transition has tense points: a buyer asks for more support, the lease takes longer than expected, financing conditions shift, staff rumors appear or diligence uncovers a messy report.

The answer should be practical. You want a broker who can explain next steps, not only celebrate interest. Ask how they document buyer activity, how they handle difficult feedback and what they recommend when market response is weaker than expected.

Broker interview scorecard

Question area Strong answer Pause if you hear
Representation The broker names who they represent, who receives confidential information and when conflicts are disclosed. "We just make the deal happen" without explaining duties or conflicts.
Fees All commissions, minimums, retainers, expenses, taxes, referral fees and holdover terms are written clearly. Only the headline commission is discussed.
Valuation The broker reviews normalized cash flow, hygiene, active patients, lease, equipment, staffing and transition assumptions. The price is based mainly on a generic multiple or what the seller wants.
Confidentiality Buyer access is staged with NDAs, controlled materials and privacy-aware handling of patient information. Practice identity or sensitive reports are shared too broadly too early.
Process Updates, buyer tracking, offer comparison and advisor coordination are defined before launch. You are told not to worry about the details until later.

How this differs from asking about broker reviews or fees

Reviews can tell you whether clients felt respected, but they rarely show the engagement terms. Fee guides can tell you what to compare, but they do not prove the broker has the right buyer strategy or valuation process. This interview scorecard sits between those two steps. Use it after you have a shortlist and before you sign.

If you are comparing broad market options, see the guide to dental practice brokers in Ontario. If you are selling, the Canadian dental practice sale checklist can help organize the file before a broker conversation. If you are buying, use the Ontario buying checklist before pursuing a specific opportunity.

When to walk away or slow down

Slow down if the broker cannot explain who they represent, resists fee transparency, discourages independent legal review, asks for a long exclusive term without clear services, shares sensitive information casually or promises a price without reviewing the underlying practice evidence. None of these signs automatically proves bad intent, but each one requires a better answer before you sign.

Also be cautious of certainty. Dental practice transitions depend on buyer demand, financing, lease terms, staff continuity, patient goodwill, tax planning, legal documents and timing. A broker can improve preparation and process, but they cannot guarantee an outcome without seeing how the market and transaction facts develop.

FAQ

What is the most important question to ask a dental practice broker?

Start by asking who the broker represents, how they are paid and whether they receive compensation from more than one party. The answer frames every later question about valuation, marketing, buyer screening and negotiation.

Should I ask a dental broker about fees before valuation?

Yes. Ask for fees, minimums, retainers, reimbursable expenses, taxes, payment timing, holdover terms and what happens if you decline an offer or the deal does not close.

Do I still need a lawyer if I use a dental practice broker?

Yes. A broker may help locate buyers or sellers and manage process, but legal documents, representation agreements, purchase agreements, lease issues and regulatory obligations should be reviewed by qualified counsel.

What should an Ontario seller ask about confidentiality?

Ask when names, location, staff details, financials and patient information are shared, who signs confidentiality agreements, how buyer access is staged and how the seller controls disclosure before staff or patients are notified.

Should buyers ask the same questions as sellers?

Yes, but from the opposite side. Buyers should ask who the broker represents, how opportunities are sourced, whether buyer advisory is included, how conflicts are handled and whether the broker can explain valuation, financing and diligence expectations without overstating certainty.
