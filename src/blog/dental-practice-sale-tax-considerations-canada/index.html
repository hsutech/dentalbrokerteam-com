Dental Practice Sale Tax Considerations Canada: 7 Checks Before You Sign

Tax planning can change how much of a dental practice sale actually reaches the seller after closing. The key is to discuss structure before price, timing and buyer expectations harden into a binding deal.

By Dental Broker Team - Published October 2, 2026

Direct answer: settle the tax structure before you agree to the deal shape

The main Canadian dental practice sale tax considerations are transaction structure, lifetime capital gains exemption eligibility, allocation between goodwill and tangible assets, GST/HST treatment, retained corporate assets, timing and how after-tax proceeds will be extracted or reinvested. These are not paperwork details to leave until closing. They can influence price, buyer appetite, financing, representations, indemnities and the closing calendar.

For many dentist-owners, the first question is whether the sale will be an asset sale, share sale or hybrid transaction. In an asset sale, the buyer purchases selected practice assets, such as equipment, leasehold improvements, patient records and goodwill. In a share sale, the buyer purchases shares of the corporation that owns the practice, which can also bring existing liabilities and corporate history. A hybrid can separate professional goodwill, real estate, shares or other assets depending on the transaction.

The best answer depends on facts. A seller may prefer a share sale if qualified small business corporation share treatment and the lifetime capital gains exemption may apply. A buyer may prefer an asset purchase for cleaner asset selection, new tax basis and narrower inherited liability. Lenders, landlords and dental regulators can also affect what is practical. Dental Broker Team helps sellers organize the valuation and transition conversation, but this article is educational only. Use a Canadian accountant and lawyer who can review your corporation, assets, buyer, province and agreement.

7 dental practice sale tax checks before you sign

1. Model asset sale, share sale and hybrid outcomes before the LOI

A dental practice buyer and seller can agree on the same headline price and still have very different after-tax outcomes depending on structure. A seller may care most about net proceeds after corporate and personal tax. A buyer may care about deductibility, financing, liability, GST/HST, lease assignment and what assets are actually being acquired. Those interests can conflict.

Before signing a letter of intent, ask your accountant to model at least the structures the buyer is likely to request. If a share sale is your preferred route, know why a buyer might resist it. If an asset sale is likely, know how that affects goodwill, equipment, inventory, receivables, working capital and distributions. If a hybrid is proposed, make sure every component has a legal and tax reason, not just a label.

This should happen alongside valuation work. The guide on dental practice sale checklist Canada explains the broader sale file, while how to value a dental practice in Ontario explains why normalized earnings and goodwill need support before negotiations start.

2. Check lifetime capital gains exemption eligibility early

The lifetime capital gains exemption can be a major planning topic when a dentist sells qualified small business corporation shares. CRA materials describe the capital gains deduction framework, and Finance Canada has confirmed the increased lifetime capital gains exemption limit with indexation. The exact result is not automatic. Eligibility depends on the shares, corporation, asset mix, ownership history, residency and other conditions.

Do not assume incorporation alone solves this. If excess cash, investments, inactive assets, shareholder loans or family ownership issues sit inside the corporation, your accountant may need time to assess whether purification or restructuring is available. Some issues are expensive or impossible to fix once an LOI, buyer diligence or closing date is already in motion.

Ask a specific question: if the proposed transaction closes on the expected date, which shares, which owner and which amount might qualify, and what evidence supports that answer? If the answer depends on proposed law, indexation, ownership timing or corporate cleanup, treat it as a planning workstream.

3. Separate goodwill, equipment, inventory and receivables

CRA's business-purchase guidance says that where a sale agreement reasonably sets prices for individual assets, those values can be used for tax purposes by the buyer. If the contract does not set them out, the parties have to decide how much of the purchase price should reasonably be attributed to assets, inventory and goodwill. That allocation should be consistent with how the vendor reports the sale.

For dental practices, goodwill often carries much of the value, but goodwill is not a magic bucket. Patient relationships, hygiene recall, location, staff continuity, brand, charts, systems and seller transition support all affect transferability. Equipment and leasehold improvements may have a different tax treatment from goodwill. Receivables, supplies and work in progress may need separate treatment.

Use valuation evidence to support allocation. The article on dental practice valuation multiples in Canada explains why simple percentages can mislead when earnings quality, buyer type and risk are not aligned.

4. Confirm GST/HST treatment instead of assuming it is exempt

Dental services may make sellers think GST/HST is simple. A practice sale can be different. CRA guidance says that when a buyer acquires all or substantially all of the property reasonably necessary to carry on a business, the buyer and seller may be able to jointly elect so that no GST/HST is payable on the sale of the business or part of a business. CRA also notes exceptions, including taxable services, certain leases or similar arrangements, and some real property situations.

That means GST/HST should be treated as a closing condition, not a vague assumption. Who prepares the election? Who files it? What if it is late, incomplete or unavailable? Does the agreement include an indemnity if CRA later disagrees? Are there assets, services, lease rights or real property components that fall outside the election?

These questions belong in the purchase agreement and advisor checklist. They should not be discovered when closing funds are already moving.

5. Review retained cash, investments, debt and corporate cleanup

A dental professional corporation may hold more than the operating practice. It might have cash, investments, vehicles, shareholder loans, equipment debt, tax balances, payroll accounts, supplier obligations or old records. In a share sale, the buyer may care about what remains inside the corporation. In an asset sale, the seller must still decide what happens to the corporation and how proceeds are extracted.

Ask your accountant and lawyer what should be cleaned up before market, before LOI and before closing. Corporate records, minute books, shareholder agreements, payroll accounts, GST/HST accounts, tax filings and debt releases may affect buyer confidence. A tax-efficient transaction can still slow down if the corporate house is not in order.

This also protects confidentiality. Buyers should receive enough information to assess the opportunity, but patient information and sensitive records need controls. For Ontario practice sales, RCDSO guidance on ownership changes and confidentiality should be built into the diligence process.

6. Match tax planning with financing, lease and buyer diligence

Tax structure does not sit by itself. A buyer's lender may prefer one structure, request specific due diligence or require clean lease assignment terms. A landlord may need to consent to assignment or new lease documents. A buyer's lawyer may push for representations, warranties, holdbacks or indemnities if a share sale exposes them to historic liabilities.

If your tax plan requires a share sale but buyers in your market consistently need asset-sale financing, that tension needs to be handled before marketing. If an asset sale creates a worse seller tax result, it may affect price expectations. If a buyer asks for a hybrid transaction, your advisors need to test whether the structure works commercially and fiscally.

The dental practice transition timeline can help sequence these issues so tax, legal, financing and lease work do not collide in the final week before closing.

7. Plan after-tax proceeds, retirement cash flow and reinvestment

A seller should not evaluate a practice sale only by gross price. Ask what reaches you after corporate tax, personal tax, debt repayment, advisor fees, holdbacks, working capital adjustments, equipment liens, lease obligations and any seller financing. Then ask when it reaches you. A higher nominal price with a risky earnout, promissory note or delayed payment can be weaker than a cleaner deal with lower uncertainty.

If you are retiring, coordinate tax planning with retirement income, registered accounts, investment policy, real estate, family planning and estate planning. If you are staying on as an associate or seller consultant, understand how compensation will be taxed and how the agreement affects your post-closing responsibilities.

The goal is not to turn a broker into a tax advisor. It is to keep the sale process honest. A good process brings tax advice in early enough that structure, valuation and buyer negotiations support the seller's real after-tax outcome.

What to ask your tax advisor before going to market

Bring practical questions, not just the proposed asking price. Ask whether your corporation appears eligible for qualified small business corporation share treatment. Ask what would change under an asset sale. Ask whether retained assets create a problem. Ask how goodwill, equipment, inventory and receivables should be allocated. Ask whether GST/HST elections, indemnities or filings are expected. Ask what records a buyer and lender will request. Ask what can still be fixed before an LOI and what is too late.

Then connect the tax answer back to your sale strategy. If your after-tax result depends on a share sale, your marketing, buyer screening and negotiation need to reflect that. If your likely buyer pool prefers asset deals, you may need a price, timing or structure conversation sooner. If the practice has strong earnings but messy corporate records, cleanup may be one of the highest-return tasks before launch.

FAQ

What is the biggest tax consideration when selling a dental practice in Canada?

The first major tax consideration is usually whether the deal is structured as an asset sale, share sale or hybrid transaction. That choice can affect who receives the proceeds, what liabilities transfer, whether the seller may qualify for the lifetime capital gains exemption, how goodwill and equipment are treated, and what the buyer can deduct after closing.

Is a dental practice asset sale or share sale better for tax?

Neither is automatically better. Sellers often prefer share sales when qualified small business corporation share treatment and the lifetime capital gains exemption may apply. Buyers often prefer asset purchases for clearer asset selection and tax basis. The right structure depends on the corporation, assets, liabilities, buyer, lender, provincial rules and advisor advice.

Does GST/HST apply to selling a dental practice?

It can, but CRA guidance says a buyer and seller may be able to make a joint election when all or substantially all of the property needed to carry on the business is acquired. Exceptions still matter, including taxable services, leases or real property in some cases. Sellers should get tax advice before assuming no GST/HST applies.

When should a dentist start tax planning before selling?

Start before a letter of intent whenever possible. Some issues, such as corporate purification, share structure, retained cash, family ownership, asset allocation and closing date, can be easier to address months or years before a binding deal than after buyer diligence has begun.

Is this tax advice?

No. This article is educational planning guidance for dental practice sellers. Dentists should rely on their own accountant, tax advisor, lawyer, lender and valuation advisor for transaction-specific advice.
