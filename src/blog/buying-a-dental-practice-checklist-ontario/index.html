Buying a Dental Practice Checklist Ontario: 9 Steps Before You Make an Offer

An Ontario dental practice buying checklist should help a buyer decide whether a target practice deserves an offer, what conditions belong in that offer and which risks need advisor review before the deal becomes hard to unwind.

Direct answer: use the checklist before the LOI narrows your options

The best time to use a buying checklist is before you sign a letter of intent or submit an offer. At that stage you may not have every document, but you should know whether the practice fits your clinical goals, location needs, financing capacity, risk tolerance and transition plan. The checklist should also tell you which conditions, advisors and document requests belong in the next step.

This article is intentionally narrower than a full dental practice due diligence checklist. Due diligence verifies documents after access is granted. A pre-offer buying checklist helps you decide whether to pursue the opportunity at all. If you are still learning the acquisition process, start with the first-time buyer guide; if capital is the main issue, read the guide to dental practice financing in Canada.

Dental Broker Team works with Ontario dentists on practice appraisals, buyer conversations and transition planning. This checklist is educational, not legal, tax, accounting, lending, privacy or clinical advice. A real transaction should be reviewed by qualified Ontario advisors who can see the actual documents.

9-step checklist before buying a dental practice in Ontario

1. Define the acquisition you can actually run

Before looking at a listing, write down the practice you can operate. Include location, commute, operatories, hours, clinical mix, patient demographics, team size, associate needs, hygiene model and seller transition expectations. A practice can look attractive on revenue and still be wrong for your skills, lifestyle or ownership stage.

This is especially important for first-time buyers. Practice ownership adds staffing, systems, marketing, lease, compliance, technology, banking and patient-retention responsibilities. The CDA buyer FAQ frames ownership as a mix of professional, financial and personal considerations. Use that broader lens before deciding that a practice is simply "a good deal."

2. Confirm financing range before you fall in love with the practice

Talk to dental-focused lenders early. You do not need final approval before you know the practice, but you should understand likely down payment, debt-service comfort, working capital, personal obligations and what documents the lender will require. Financing is not only about headline price. It is affected by lease security, normalized earnings, equipment needs, buyer income expectations and transaction structure.

If government-supported financing is part of the conversation, confirm eligibility with the lender before relying on it. CSBFP guidance says the purchase of eligible assets of an existing business may qualify, but share purchases and assets acquired by a holding company are not eligible under that program. That distinction can matter before structure gets negotiated.

3. Decide whether the opportunity deserves confidential access

A seller should not release sensitive details to every curious buyer. A serious buyer should be ready with acquisition criteria, advisor contacts, financing direction and a signed confidentiality agreement. Before asking for deeper information, decide what you need to see to make the next decision: summary financials, production mix, lease basics, operatories, equipment age, staff profile, active-patient indicators and seller transition expectations.

Use the dental practice listings page to understand opportunity signals, but do not confuse a teaser with an investment case. The teaser gets you interested. The checklist tells you whether to proceed.

4. Separate valuation logic from asking price

An asking price is a proposal. Valuation logic is the evidence behind it. Ask how earnings were normalized, what owner adjustments were made, whether revenue depends on the seller, how hygiene contributes, whether collections are stable and how equipment or lease risk was reflected. If the price assumes growth, identify what has to happen after closing to make that growth realistic.

For more depth, compare the opportunity with the guide on how to value a dental practice in Ontario and the broader article on dental practice valuation multiples in Canada. Do not let a multiple replace judgment about transferability.

5. Review lease risk before the offer gets specific

Dental goodwill is tied to location. Before an offer hardens, understand remaining lease term, renewal options, assignment requirements, rent escalations, demolition or relocation rights, parking, signage, exclusivity and landlord consent. Delta Law's checklist treats lease review as a major acquisition issue because a buyer may struggle to operate in the existing location if the lease cannot be assigned or extended.

If the seller owns the building, keep the practice purchase and real estate decision separate until your advisors model both. Buying the practice may be attractive while buying the building is premature, or the opposite may be true.

6. Check patient-base and hygiene signals without overreaching

Ask for aggregated indicators first: active patient definition, new-patient flow, hygiene recall, provider production, service mix, payer mix, cancellations, no-shows and unscheduled treatment. Patient count is only useful when you know how it was calculated. A file that counts anyone seen in five years is not the same as a file built on recent active recall.

Be disciplined with privacy. RCDSO guidance says personal health information can be disclosed to a potential purchaser for assessment only after a confidentiality agreement is in place. Many early questions can be answered with anonymized or aggregated reports before identifiable records are required.

7. Understand what you are buying: assets, shares, goodwill and obligations

Ontario dental acquisitions commonly raise asset-versus-share questions. Delta Law's overview explains that an asset purchase involves selected clinic assets, while a share purchase means buying the corporation that owns the practice, including its existing assets and liabilities. Tax, liability, contracts, employees, patient records and financing can all push the structure one way or another.

At the checklist stage, do not try to solve structure alone. Instead, identify which structure the seller expects, whether your lender can support it and which advisors need to comment before the LOI. A structure mismatch can waste weeks if it appears late.

8. Test team, associate and seller-transition fit

The team carries much of the practice's goodwill. Ask about staff roles, tenure, compensation, benefits, vacation, associate agreements, contractor relationships and any expected departures. Also ask what role the seller will play after closing. Will they introduce patients, stay clinically for a period, reduce gradually or leave immediately?

CDA buyer guidance emphasizes culture, team relationships and gradual change. That is practical advice, not a soft detail. A financially sound practice can stumble if the team does not trust the transition or if the buyer changes too much too quickly.

9. Convert the checklist into offer conditions

The checklist should produce an action, not just notes. If the opportunity still fits, turn open risks into offer conditions and advisor assignments. Typical conditions may involve financing, legal review, accounting review, lease assignment or extension, satisfactory diligence, equipment verification, employment or associate review and transition terms. If too many core questions remain unanswered, wait.

Good buyers move with discipline. They are responsive, clear and serious, but they do not remove protection before the facts support it. When to pause before making an offer

Pause if the seller cannot explain the numbers, if active patient definitions keep changing, if the lease term is too short, if staff stability is unclear, if equipment obligations are vague, if financing depends on optimistic growth or if you are being rushed around advisor review. None of those issues automatically kills a purchase, but each needs an answer before you trade flexibility for momentum.

Also pause if the practice is attractive only because it is available. Opportunity scarcity can make buyers soften their standards. A stronger approach is to define the practice you can run, then use each listing as evidence for or against that fit.

FAQ

What should I check before buying a dental practice in Ontario?

Check buyer fit, financing capacity, valuation logic, lease security, active patient and hygiene signals, staff and associate continuity, equipment needs, privacy controls and the seller transition plan before submitting an offer or removing conditions.

Is this checklist the same as due diligence?

No. This checklist is for pre-offer and offer-condition decisions. Formal due diligence is deeper document verification after confidentiality, access and timelines are agreed.

Can a buyer see patient records before closing in Ontario?

Ontario guidance from RCDSO says a seller may disclose personal health information to a potential purchaser for assessment only after the potential purchaser enters into a confidentiality agreement. Use aggregated information first when possible and confirm privacy rules with counsel.

Should financing be arranged before making an offer?

A buyer should speak with lenders and understand likely capacity before making an offer. Approval still depends on the buyer, practice, purchase terms, lease, valuation support and lender requirements.

Is this legal, tax, lending or privacy advice?

No. It is educational acquisition guidance. Buyers should rely on qualified Ontario legal, tax, accounting, valuation, privacy and lending advisors for a specific transaction.
