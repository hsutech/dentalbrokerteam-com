Preparing a Dental Practice for Sale: 7 Priorities Before Listing

Decide which improvements deserve attention before marketing your practice, and which investments need a stronger case.

By Dental Broker Team · Published October 16, 2026

Start with the problem a buyer cannot easily fix

Preparing a dental practice for sale means making its earnings, operations and handover easier to assess before you invite offers. Start with unresolved lease issues, inconsistent reporting, owner dependence and advice on sale structure. Keep necessary maintenance and patient care on track. Treat optional renovations and new equipment as separate investment decisions, with no assumption that a buyer will reimburse their cost.

For an Ontario owner deciding whether to list now or spend time preparing, the useful question is: which change removes a real obstacle to a transaction? A freshly decorated reception area will not explain a revenue discrepancy. A new scanner will not resolve uncertain premises rights. The seven priorities below are an editorial decision framework, not a ranking based on measured sale-price returns.

This guide focuses on improvement decisions before marketing. For the records to assemble once those decisions are made, use the Canadian dental practice sale checklist. The recommendations here are general education; your lawyer, accountant and valuation advisor should assess the circumstances of a particular sale.

7 improvements to consider before listing

1. Make recurring earnings understandable

Ask your accountant to reconcile financial statements with practice-management reports for matching periods. Production, collections, deposits and taxable income are different measures. Keep them labelled, and explain adjustments instead of presenting whichever figure looks strongest. A buyer should be able to follow a number back to its source.

Create a short change log for unusual events: an associate leaving, temporary closure, equipment repair or altered clinical hours. Record when the event happened and which figures it affected. Separate genuine one-off expenditure from costs a successor would still need to pay. If an expense is disputed, preserve the original figure alongside the proposed adjustment.

The practical improvement is consistency. Choose a monthly reporting process that can continue during marketing, so a buyer does not receive a different definition of earnings each time. Before paying for an expansion to improve the headline numbers, establish a baseline through a dental practice appraisal. Ask which assumptions require stronger evidence, rather than asking for a desired valuation.

2. Investigate the lease before renovating the premises

Find the signed lease, amendments and renewal correspondence. Ask your lawyer which provisions matter for the proposed sale, including assignment, consent, renewal and any change-of-control terms. Do not rely on a recollection that the landlord is supportive. Establish who must take the next step and what information they will need.

Delta Law's Ontario sale guide identifies remaining term, renewal options, rent increases and assignment rights as lease-review issues. The right response depends on the actual agreement. A seller should not promise a particular outcome before that review.

Our suggested spending test is simple: would this premises improvement still make sense if the proposed buyer or closing date changed? Obtain advice before a major fit-out, extension or new obligation. If you own the building, decide whether it is part of the transaction or a separate discussion; that choice changes what a prospective buyer needs to evaluate.

3. Make routine operations less dependent on you

List the administrative tasks that currently require the owner: supplier approvals, payroll questions, schedule changes, software access and responding to equipment failures. For each task, identify a responsible person, an appropriate backup and the information they need. This is an operational exercise, not a reason to disclose a possible sale prematurely.

BDC's business-sale preparation guidance highlights repeatable processes and employee responsibility. In a dental practice, a practical application is to document ordinary administrative work without blurring clinical responsibilities or granting inappropriate access to patient information.

Test the instructions during normal planned absences and note where work still stalls. Do not manufacture independence by suddenly withdrawing from clinical work just before marketing. Instead, distinguish tasks the team can manage from services that depend on your skills or relationships. A clear statement of those dependencies helps frame a realistic workback discussion with a buyer.

4. Clarify patient activity without chasing a cosmetic spike

A patient total is difficult to interpret without a definition. Record the period used for an active-patient report, whether duplicate records are excluded and whether the same method was used in previous periods. Review appointment availability, cancellations and hygiene scheduling with your practice manager using appropriately restricted data.

Choose improvements that serve patients and can continue after a sale. For example, if a cancellation list is maintained inconsistently, establish responsibility for keeping it current. If appointment reminders are unreliable, investigate the process. These are workflow suggestions, not claims that a particular intervention will increase practice value.

Keep clinical recommendations based on patient need. Avoid aggressive discounting or changes designed only to create a short-lived production increase. When you try an administrative improvement, record its start date and assess comparable periods. Buyers can then distinguish an established pattern from a recent experiment, and you can discuss both without overstating what the data proves.

5. Separate necessary maintenance from speculative upgrades

Build an equipment schedule showing what is owned, financed, leased or supplied under another arrangement. Note service history, known defects and any commitments connected with software or maintenance. Obtain qualified technical advice where necessary; photographs cannot establish equipment condition or regulatory compliance.

Maintain safe, reliable operations throughout the sale process. For optional spending, compare three choices: repair the existing item, replace it for an operational reason, or disclose its condition and leave the future choice open. Consider downtime, staff training, ongoing subscriptions and financing obligations alongside the purchase cost.

A buyer may prefer a different technology or workflow. Consequently, do not assume a new scanner, chair or reception renovation adds its invoice value to the sale price. Ask the valuator how the proposed expenditure would affect the assessment and what remains uncertain. A written repair estimate and honest condition record can support a clearer discussion than an unsupported claim that the clinic is fully updated.

6. Get advice on structure before moving corporate assets

Bring your accountant and lawyer into the preparation stage before agreeing to transaction terms. Explain your preferred exit, any continuing clinical role and whether the corporation holds assets unrelated to the practice. Ask them to identify decisions that need lead time and actions that should wait until the transaction is better defined.

BDO Canada's dental sale preparation article discusses corporate purification and the importance of planning. Its worked tax examples reference earlier years, so they should not be treated as a current tax quotation. The useful preparation lesson is to obtain advice early; do not transfer investments or reorganize a corporation using a generic checklist.

Request a comparison of feasible structures and the information still needed to assess them. Keep gross price, transaction expenses, debt obligations and estimated after-tax proceeds distinct in your planning. This article does not calculate an exemption, tax rate or expected net outcome. Those depend on the transaction and the rules applicable when it occurs.

7. Make confidentiality a working process

Create separate stages for initial interest, qualified buyer review and detailed diligence. Decide who approves access, which documents belong at each stage and how outdated versions are withdrawn. Keep a record of what was released, to whom and when. A shared folder with unrestricted access is not a disclosure plan.

The RCDSO ownership-change FAQ says that before disclosing personal health information to a potential purchaser for assessing the practice, the custodian must have the purchaser enter a confidentiality agreement. It also addresses written patient notification by the selling dentist. Have your advisors apply the requirements to the proposed disclosure and transfer; an agreement is not permission for unrestricted sharing.

Keep early materials focused on what is necessary for the decision at that stage. Review redactions before release and avoid moving patient information into ordinary marketing documents. Assign responsibility for patient and staff communications so that preparation remains controlled as discussions progress.

Choose what to fix now and what to defer

For each proposed improvement, write down its purpose, expected cost, responsible person, dependencies and evidence of completion. Ask whether it removes a sale obstacle, improves ongoing operations or simply changes presentation. There is no universal preparation budget or guaranteed return for any of these categories.

Consider a hypothetical practice with inconsistent financial reports, a lease question and dated but serviceable reception furniture. Investigating the first two issues would clarify whether the transaction is feasible. Replacing the furniture would answer a different question. This example illustrates prioritization; it does not predict a buyer's offer or imply that all cosmetic work is unnecessary.

If you need to sell promptly, disclose unresolved issues accurately and ask advisors which can be addressed within the process. If you have more flexibility, agree on measurable preparation goals before delaying the sale. Revisit the decision when those goals are met. For transaction stages after preparation, see the dental practice transition timeline.

When is the practice ready for a market discussion?

You do not need a flawless business. You need a coherent account of what is being sold, reliable supporting information and a plan for the material uncertainties. Ask an advisor to read the package as a buyer would: what cannot be verified, what depends on you staying, and what decision is being left until too late?

Bring specific questions about priorities, scope and responsibilities rather than assuming every suggested improvement needs to be completed.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do first when preparing a dental practice for sale?

Start with a confidential review of your objectives, financial reporting and any material lease or operating issues. Establish a valuation baseline and ask your advisors which uncertainties should be resolved before marketing.

Should I buy new equipment before selling?

Maintain equipment needed for safe, reliable care. Assess optional purchases individually, including cost, downtime and ongoing commitments. Do not assume a buyer will pay back the purchase cost through a higher offer.

Can I prepare without telling everyone the practice is for sale?

Some preparation, such as improving reporting and documenting routine work, can be part of ordinary management. Plan sale-related disclosure and communications with advisors, including applicable patient-notification and privacy requirements.

How much should I spend on sale preparation?

There is no standard amount that suits every practice. Obtain scoped estimates for identified problems and distinguish necessary operating expenditure from discretionary improvements. Compare the remaining uncertainties before approving a major project.

Does better preparation guarantee a higher sale price?

No. Preparation can make information clearer and address problems, but price and completion still depend on the practice, buyer, terms and market. Record actual improvements without promising a valuation increase or a closing date.
